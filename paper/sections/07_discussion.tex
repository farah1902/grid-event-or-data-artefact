\section{Discussion}\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{Answers to the research questions}\label{sec:answers}

Table~\ref{tab:answers} answers the research questions and hypotheses of Section~\ref{sec:intro}. Three results reach beyond this dataset. First, most of what looks like an outage in this telemetry comes from the acquisition chain: holds, straight lines, and zeros in several cities at once. A study that reads every zero as an outage, or interpolates every gap, measures its data pipeline as much as its grid. Second, the evidence that separates the two is structural rather than dynamic. Simultaneity, exact repetition and collinearity carry it, whereas the shape of the restoration, which the typology expected to be decisive, carries almost none: an apparent ramp is more often an interpolated gap than a cold-load pick-up. Third, weak supervision earns its place through stability and calibration rather than accuracy. On clean signatures a majority vote of the rules is as accurate, but only the constrained label model gives calibrated probabilities and labels that do not depend on the random start.

The two public copies of the data, the UCI file analysed here \cite{bensalah2025uci} and the files of the data article \cite{bensalah2025dib}, hold the same series, the second rounded to two decimals; both contain these artefacts. The forecasting study that introduced the data reports its own preprocessing \cite{bensalah2025opsearch}, and a later study aggregates the zones into city series for demand scheduling \cite{sarange2026}. We did not re-examine how these studies treated anomalies and make no claim about their results. Wherever the series are used without typing their anomalies, however, error metrics mix acquisition failures with load behaviour; the released catalogue lets future studies impute the artefacts and keep the grid events. More generally, load-curve cleansing repairs corrupted and missing readings caused by meter and transfer failures \cite{chen2010cleansing}, and SCADA cleaning removes anomalous points before modelling \cite{morrison2022scada}. Both treat anomalies as errors to repair or remove, whereas grid events are information to keep.

\subsection{Implications for distribution operators}\label{sec:implications}

\emph{Reliability reporting.} Counting the telemetry's zeros as outages would overstate feeder outage time 4.15-fold, and on injected data typed counting reduces the error from 1.88 to 1.26 times the truth. Customer-weighted reliability indices need customer numbers per feeder, which the dataset does not give, but they are built from the same outage durations. Dead states should therefore be typed before indices are computed from feeder telemetry, and simultaneous zeros across cities should be treated as an acquisition problem until shown otherwise.

\emph{Telemetry quality.} Holds and straight-line fills are cheap to test where the data enter the system (exact repetition, constant first difference), and they point to the recording and export chain rather than to the network. Such a test would flag almost half of the items found here (155 of 327) before they reach an operator's display or a forecaster.

\emph{Forecasting pipelines.} Interpolating an outage away helps only a forecaster that cannot be told about it. A model that reads an event indicator forecasts better when grid events are kept and marked, so cleaning should pass the types to the forecaster rather than only remove anomalies.
\subsection{Limitations}\label{sec:limitations}

The labels on the real data are not validated against utility outage records. Their accuracy is known only on injected events, and their 98.2\% agreement with majority vote measures consistency, not accuracy. The benchmark favours the rules: the injected artefacts copy the episodes from which the rules were written, the grid-event physics is hypothesised, and the injected zero gaps copy only the zero part of the real compound episodes, which explains part of the A1-to-G1 confusion. Injected results therefore measure performance on these signatures, not on unseen kinds of event. Real events are few, with no regional outage, one city outage and one load transfer, so per-class statistics on the real data are impossible.

The thresholds were set on the same data. On injected data the rules are insensitive to them, except for a hold length of four steps (rule F1 0.765 instead of 0.891) and a dead threshold of 2\% (label-model macro-F1 0.819 instead of 0.906); the defining-rule prior changes 39\% of the dead labels. The item-level label model ignores episode context, as the last item of Fig.~\ref{fig:mar2024} shows. The detector is retrospective, its onsets lag by 2--3~h, and many of its extra alarms fall on neighbouring zones. The transfer test uses one small, clean city, in which zero gaps and city outages cannot be told apart. Time stamps are used as recorded, so the planned-work rule follows the two calendars of Section~\ref{sec:res-telemetry}. Marrakech lies outside the injection benchmark, and all 48 of its dead items lie in one small zone, 44 of them noise-floor runs, so its labels are the least certain.

Larger transformer-based detectors were not run; GDN and USAD represent the deep forecasting and reconstruction families, and the unconstrained Dawid--Skene model and majority vote stand in for other label models. GDN, run as released, forecasts these feeders worse than the last value, so its result is no verdict on graph detectors. Two analyses, the graph ablation and the deep baselines, were added after the test results had been seen; neither changed a design choice. Further baselines can be added on the same seeded datasets; a design change suggested by the test results would need fresh seeds.
