\section{Related work}\label{sec:related}

\subsection{Anomalies in energy and metering data}\label{sec:rw-energy}

Anomaly detection in energy consumption has been reviewed for buildings \cite{himeur2021review}, where unsupervised methods such as autoencoders are preferred because anomaly labels are rarely available \cite{fan2018autoencoder}. In distribution networks, smart-meter data have been used to detect anomalous events at lateral and customer levels with a generative model that accounts for the hierarchical structure of the network \cite{moghaddass2018hierarchical}, and outage management locates the faulted section from smart-meter outage notifications and fault-indicator flags, allowing for reports lost to firmware, software or communication failures \cite{jiang2016outage}. A separate line treats faulty readings as bad data to be repaired or removed: smoothing that cleanses corrupted and missing load-curve data \cite{chen2010cleansing}, a robust low-rank and sparse decomposition that attributes outliers to faulty readings and attacks and missing data to communication errors \cite{mateos2013cleansing}, and anomaly filters that clean wind-turbine SCADA power curves \cite{morrison2022scada}. Repair leaves its own trace: in a power-industry best practice, smart-meter gaps shorter than two hours are imputed by linear interpolation \cite{peppanen2016imputation}, and a gap filled so becomes a straight line, one of the fill signatures typed here. Readings can also be wrong by design: a recent review of smart-grid security covers data manipulation among attacks on SCADA systems, IoT devices and energy management systems \cite{hassine2025security}; deliberate manipulation is left to future work. In feeder telemetry, unusual readings and wrong readings occur together and call for opposite treatment: an acquisition artefact should be imputed, a grid event kept.

\subsection{Bad data versus events in grid measurements}\label{sec:rw-pmu}

The closest problem is known for synchrophasor measurements in transmission grids, where bad data must be told from real events. Low-quality measurements have been detected as spatio-temporal outliers, from the similarity of measurements across sites and over time, under both normal and fault-on conditions \cite{wu2017lowquality}, and an isolation-forest detector with two levels of hierarchical subspace features separates events from low-quality data and so avoids false alarms \cite{wu2021iforest}. The logic is close to our simultaneity rules: whether an anomaly is shared across sensors is evidence about its cause. Our setting differs in four ways: ten-minute feeder telemetry instead of synchrophasor streams; structured artefacts from the acquisition and export chain (held values, straight lines, zero gaps) instead of noisy or outlying readings; a typed label with a calibrated probability instead of a flag for bad data or events; and no labels at any stage.

\subsection{Weak supervision}\label{sec:rw-ws}

When no ground truth exists, labels can be inferred from several noisy sources. The Dawid--Skene model estimates each source's confusion matrix and the true classes jointly by expectation--maximisation \cite{dawid1979}, and spectral initialisation gives provable guarantees, including for the one-coin model in which each source has a single accuracy \cite{zhang2016spectral}. Data programming lets labelling functions vote, possibly in conflict, and trains a discriminative model on the denoised labels \cite{ratner2016dataprogramming}; Snorkel makes the approach practical \cite{ratner2017snorkel}, and its label model recovers the accuracies of abstaining sources by matrix completion, without labels \cite{ratner2019metal}. In power systems, labelling functions have classified PMU events in transmission grids from a small labelled share \cite{liu2022weakly,liu2025automatic}, after data-quality issues were fixed \cite{liu2022weakly}. Here the data-quality issues are what is labelled, and no labels are used. Our rules also differ from generic labelling functions: each votes for one class or one family of classes, applies only to some kinds of item, and most items receive one to three votes. Section~\ref{sec:dsfail} shows why a free Dawid--Skene model is unstable on such votes, and Snorkel's label model, run on the same votes, is less stable and less accurate than the constrained model of Section~\ref{sec:labelmodel} (Section~\ref{sec:res-attribution}). The probabilistic labels are also the product here, not only training data, so their calibration matters in itself.

\subsection{Multivariate and graph-based detection}\label{sec:rw-mts}

Multivariate detectors learn normal behaviour and flag deviations from it: forecasting-based detectors threshold the residual of a predictor \cite{hundman2018spacecraft}, reconstruction-based ones score what an encoder cannot reproduce \cite{su2019omnianomaly,audibert2020usad}, and graph-based ones relate the variables through graph attention \cite{zhao2020mtadgat,velickovic2018gat} or through a graph learned from sensor embeddings \cite{deng2021gdn}. Temporal convolutional networks \cite{bai2018tcn} and wavelet decompositions \cite{percival2000wavelet} provide the multi-scale views used in this paper. These detectors return a score, not a type, and a held value or a straight line is easy to forecast and to reconstruct, so it can look normal to them (Section~\ref{sec:res-detection}).

\subsection{Evaluating anomaly detection}\label{sec:rw-eval}

How detections are counted changes the ranking of methods: point adjustment can make even a random score look competitive \cite{kim2022rigorous}, range-based precision and recall account for anomalies that last over time \cite{tatbul2018precision}, and popular benchmarks suffer from trivial anomalies, unrealistic anomaly density, mislabelled ground truth and run-to-failure bias \cite{wu2023flawed}. Calibration is evaluated separately: modern neural networks are often poorly calibrated, and temperature scaling corrects them on held-out labelled data \cite{guo2017calibration}; without labels, calibration can only be measured on injected events. This paper counts events rather than adjusted points, reports several counting views, and injects only signatures observed in the data, which leaves the circularity discussed in Section~\ref{sec:limitations}.

\subsection{Positioning}\label{sec:positioning}

Table~\ref{tab:positioning} summarises the closest works. The capability this paper adds is one: typing each anomaly of a feeder record as an acquisition artefact or a grid event, with a calibrated probability and without labelled data. The closest works address neighbouring problems: separating bad data from events in transmission measurements \cite{wu2017lowquality,wu2021iforest}, detecting anomalous events and locating outages from smart meters \cite{moghaddass2018hierarchical,jiang2016outage}, and classifying PMU events by weak supervision once data quality has been fixed \cite{liu2022weakly}. On top of the typing, the paper uses the acquisition chain as a level of the hierarchy, evaluates the catalogue on outage statistics and forecasts, and tests transfer to a second system.
